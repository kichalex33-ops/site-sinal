Summary

In the early hours of August 15, 1977, the Big Ear radio telescope at Ohio State University recorded a narrowband radio signal far stronger than the background noise of the sky, at a frequency close to the 21 cm line of neutral hydrogen — historically one of the most promising bands for searches of artificial signals. The signal lasted 72 seconds, the exact time it took Earth's rotation to carry the instrument's beam across that point in the sky, and it appeared in only one of the telescope's two receiving channels, as would be expected from a real source rather than terrestrial interference. Days later, while reviewing the printed computer printout, astronomer Jerry Ehman circled the sequence "6EQUJ5" and wrote "Wow!" in the margin — the origin of the name by which the case is known. There has never been a second detection, despite decades of searches aimed at the same region of the sky with more sensitive instruments, and no raw data exists beyond that single printout.

What happened, in a few lines, with no interpretation built in.

Hypotheses

All in the same field of evaluation. None wins by default.

Astrophysical

Recent studies ("Arecibo Wow! I" and "Arecibo Wow! II", 2024–2025) propose a cold neutral-hydrogen cloud amplified by a rare transient event. This would explain the frequency, but neither paper has reached consensus in the scientific community.

Instrumental error

Terrestrial interference was considered and partly ruled out: Big Ear had two receiving beams, and the signal appeared in only one, as would be expected from a real celestial source rather than local noise common to both beams.

Extraordinary hypothesis

A non-natural, technological origin is often cited, but no additional data (repetition, message structure, a second detection) supports it beyond the anomaly of the signal itself.

Contradictions
  • The signal was never redetected, despite dozens of attempts with more sensitive instruments in the following decades.
  • No audio recording or raw data exists beyond the printout — the analysis depends entirely on that single record.
What we know
  • A real radio signal was recorded by the instrument on 08/15/1977, with an intensity profile consistent with a fixed source in the sky.
  • The frequency was close to the 21 cm line of neutral hydrogen, of historical interest to SETI searches.
What we don't know
  • The physical origin of the signal — natural, artificial or instrumental.
  • Why it was never repeated, even with targeted searches.
What we'd need to know
  • Additional raw data from the moment of detection, if it exists in any archive, has not yet been published.
  • A second detection, at the same frequency and region of the sky, would be the single most informative piece of data possible.
